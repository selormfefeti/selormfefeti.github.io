02  /  Case study

Intapp2021 – 2026Platform Product Manager

From “we have logs” to “we can prove control.”

  • ProductAudit Trail for Read Events — tracks user interaction with sensitive data down to the object and field level
  • RoleProduct lead for the enterprise client solutions suite; owned the roadmap
  • UsersCompliance officers and administrators at global financial services firms
  • DriverMarket Abuse Regulation, including a seven-year retention obligation
  • OutcomeFlagship capability in the enterprise compliance suite, which generated 30% of participating client ARR

The situation

Compliance officers at financial services firms had transactional logs, but the logs were hard to query. They could see that an event had happened without seeing who accessed which sensitive data, or whether that access fit the person’s normal pattern.

For firms subject to the Market Abuse Regulation, that gap mattered. Insider threats and market abuse surface first as unusual data access, and early detection of it wasn’t something the existing tooling was built to support. Review happened after the fact. That doesn’t answer the question a regulator actually asks in an exam: how would you have caught this?

Before

Transactional logs, hard to query. The event was visible. The actor, the field they touched, and whether any of it was normal for them were not. Detection was retrospective.

After

Field-level visibility into who read what, ranked by deviation from that user’s normal scope and cadence. Detection moved earlier, and the answer to an examiner became defensible.

How I framed it

I worked directly with compliance officers at client firms to pin down what “suspicious” means in regulatory terms rather than in the abstract. The answer came back consistent: access that deviates from a user’s normal scope or cadence. Turning that into requirements engineering could build against was the first job.

The second job was the harder one, and it wasn’t the tracking. It was making the signal usable. Flag everything and compliance officers drown in noise, stop trusting the tool, and fall back to the after-the-fact review we were trying to replace.

Decisions and tradeoffs

Ranking over coverage

Rather than surface everything we could technically see, I prioritized which access patterns mattered most and worked with engineering on how anomalies were surfaced and ranked. Then I iterated against real reviewer behavior: what compliance officers acted on versus what they scrolled past. A signal being ignored is a product verdict, and it was the most honest data we had.

ClickHouse over the conventional database

Architecting the data layer, engineering favored a conventional database structure the team already had deep operational experience running. I pushed for ClickHouse instead, on the strength of WORM (write-once-read-many) storage, for three reasons:

Regulatory defensibility. Immutability is the whole point. If the records can be changed, they don’t prove anything.

Query performance at scale. A compliance officer investigating field-level access across a large client dataset can’t wait on a slow query mid-investigation. If the query is slow, people stop using it mid-investigation, which is exactly when they need it.

Seven-year retention. The retention period the regulators require set the storage profile, and it wasn’t one the conventional structure was going to carry well.

The cost was real. I was asking the team to take on operational unfamiliarity in exchange for properties the regulation demanded.

Result

What shipped moved administrators and compliance officers from “we have logs” to “we can prove control”: early detection of potential breaches and insider threats, field-level visibility for regulatory audits, and a defensible answer to exactly the kind of question a Market Abuse Regulation exam asks.

The sharpest measure of what that was worth isn’t a percentage. The capability was a condition of a major enterprise deal — without it, that client wouldn’t have signed. It went on to become one of the flagship capabilities in the enterprise compliance suite, and the audit trail and activity-tracking work was a major driver behind that suite generating 30% of participating client ARR.

03  /  Case study

Intapp2021 – 2026Platform Product Manager

Weeks to seconds, without handing over the keys.

  • ProductClient-facing self-service portal for site management and backend operations
  • RoleProduct lead — discovery, access model, launch
  • UsersPlatform administrators, and the security and technology officers who had to approve them
  • FindingThe binding requirement was authorization, not speed
  • OutcomeProvisioning went from weeks to seconds; hosting overhead became measurable

The situation

Platform administrators couldn’t change their own environments. Standing up a new UAT, sandbox, or production environment, toggling feature availability across production and UAT sites, setting IP restrictions — every one of these had to be routed through an internal fulfillment process that could take weeks.

It was slow and error-prone in both directions. The administrator waited on a change that should have been straightforward, and the request travelled through hands with no context on why it was being made. Every client added to the queue made the bottleneck worse.

Before

Environment provisioning, feature toggles, and IP restrictions routed through internal fulfillment. Weeks of latency, errors in handoff, and a support queue that grew with every client added.

After

Administrators configure their own environments in seconds, inside an access model that requires explicit authorization for high-stakes changes. Intapp gained a live view of active sites and their hosting cost.

What discovery actually turned up

I grounded the discovery process in direct research with security and technology officers at large-scale enterprise clients. What they told us shaped the product more than any internal assumption had.

The obvious brief for a self-service tool is to remove friction. That wasn’t the brief these customers gave. Their most critical design requirement was that the wrong person should never be able to make a high-stakes configuration change without explicit authorization. They wanted the speed, but authorization was the condition for adopting it at all. Get that ordering backwards and you ship a portal that enterprise security teams quietly decline to turn on.

Decisions and tradeoffs

The access model was the product

I carried the research findings straight into the design, engineering, and security conversations rather than converting them into a feature list first, and worked with those teams to map the admin access models and controls. I worked out the access model first, then designed the interface around it. Deciding who may do what, and what proof is required before they do it, was the actual design work.

Result

Environment provisioning compressed from a weeks-long fulfillment process to seconds. Site management moved out of the support queue and into the hands of the people who actually owned the platforms.

The third outcome was the one nobody asked for. Because the portal knew which sites were active, it gave Intapp visibility into them and their associated hosting costs. That turned operational overhead that hadn’t previously been visible into something measurable and manageable.

04  /  Case study

Compliance Solutions Strategies2017 – 2021Product Manager

Six months to fix what nobody had asked about.

  • ProductThe data repository behind the firm’s Shares Outstanding product
  • TriggerSelf-initiated — not assigned, not on any roadmap
  • ProblemA single, narrowly sourced figure carrying more downstream weight than its design supported
  • AskA six-month rearchitecture, against significant internal resistance
  • RoleFound it, built the case, stayed through delivery
  • OutcomeThe corrected model became the firm’s proprietary ML data repository, later the basis of a $300K revenue line

What I found

The existing repository produced a single figure, drawn from a narrow set of sources and refreshed infrequently. For a shares outstanding product, that figure is the denominator. Positions are measured against regulatory thresholds using it, so how it is sourced and how often it moves propagates into every percentage derived from it. The architecture had been designed for a lighter use than the one it had grown into, and the distance between those two was where the risk sat.

How I found it

Nobody assigned this. When I join a company I make a point of understanding its architecture and then walking it from a client’s point of view, looking for the distance between what the system produces and what a client is relying on it to prove. That is what surfaced this.

Making the case

The resistance came from senior technical and business stakeholders, and the objection was reasonable on its face: redesigning an architecture is expensive, and nothing was visibly broken.

So I stopped arguing about architecture and built the exhibit instead. I took a figure from the existing repository and set it beside the figure I calculated from additional data sources, then measured both against the kind of thresholds the number exists to test. The difference between them was material at exactly the point where it mattered most.

That comparison ended the debate. Once the difference sat on a page next to a threshold, the question was no longer whether the rearchitecture was worth six months of engineering.

Delivery

Approval came first, from the key stakeholders, and work started only once it was in hand. The senior technical stakeholder who had led the opposition then partnered on the build directly rather than delegating it. We worked hand in hand through testing and data scraping across the migration.

Result

The rearchitecture did more than remove the risk. The corrected model became the firm’s proprietary machine learning data repository. That became the asset the legacy Shares Outstanding product was repositioned around, generating $300K in annual revenue within twelve months.

It turned out the fix and the new revenue line were the same piece of work.

05  /  Working with AI

AI in the workflow, not layered on top of it.

Claude is my thinking partner for pressure-testing my own reasoning, catching gaps in requirements before they reach engineering, and synthesizing complex context fast. Four places it does real work:

Product definition

Drafting PRDs and then attacking them: what is ambiguous, what is unstated, what an engineer would otherwise have to guess. It is far cheaper to find those gaps before engineering starts than after.

Customer research

Synthesizing interviews, support threads, and call notes into patterns, then having the model argue against the pattern instead of confirming it. In compliance, what a customer asks for and what they actually need are often different things.

GTM strategy

Segmentation, positioning, pricing narratives, and competitive teardowns, with the model taking the buyer’s side of the argument so the weak parts surface before a prospect finds them.

My own pipeline

I run my job search on a tool I built. It pulls postings from company job boards into a local database, dedupes them, and scores each role against my background with an LLM evaluator.

Tooling

I work with skills, connectors, MCP servers, and agents, composing them into workflows rather than prompting one thing at a time. That is also how I think about building AI products: most of the hard problems live at the seams between the model, the tools it can call, and the data it is allowed to reach.
